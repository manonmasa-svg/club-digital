> **In short:**
> 1. **Phishing** is a fraudulent email, text message or call that impersonates a trusted party to obtain login details, passwords or bank details.
> 2. Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr, the French government's cyber assistance service, lists 12 warning signs, including an odd sender address, an alarming subject, an unusual request and pressure to click a link or open an attachment.
> 3. The basic habits: never share a password or bank details by message, hover over links before clicking, check the website address, contact the sender through another channel and enable two-factor authentication.
> 4. After a click, change passwords, block cards if needed, keep evidence and report the message: Signal Spam for emails, 33700 for text messages, Phishing Initiative for websites.

## What is phishing in a business context?

**Phishing in a business** takes the form of a message imitating a familiar contact: a bank, supplier, public authority, email provider, client or even a colleague. Its goal is to obtain login details, passwords or bank details, or to get someone to open a booby-trapped attachment.

According to [Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr's phishing fact sheet](https://www.cybermalveillance.gouv.fr/tous-nos-contenus/fiches-reflexes/hameconnage-phishing) (in French, updated on 26 October 2026, consulted on 16 November 2026), it can be a fake message, text message or phone call posing as a bank, social network, phone operator, energy supplier, online shop or public authority.

For a business, the stakes go beyond one employee's account. A single stolen login can open the email system, the invoicing software or the bank accounts of the whole organisation.

### Spear phishing, targeted phishing

**Spear phishing** is the most dangerous form for a business. Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr describes it as tailor-made phishing: it impersonates a person or organisation the recipient knows, with a subject and content consistent with their work.

This highly sophisticated message aims to get the recipient to open an attachment or click a malicious link. It can allow the attacker to infiltrate the victim's device and then, from there, the organisation's information system.

## 12 signs that help spot a phishing email

Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr's page on [how to recognise a phishing email](https://www.cybermalveillance.gouv.fr/tous-nos-contenus/actualites/comment-reconnaitre-un-mail-de-phishing-ou-dhameconnage) (in French, updated on 12 February 2026) lists the following warning signs, without claiming to be exhaustive.

| Warning sign | What to check |
|---|---|
| 1. A warning from the email client or antivirus | Do not ignore it, keep the antivirus on and up to date |
| 2. A service the business is not a customer of | Be wary of messages from unknown companies |
| 3. An unusual sender name | Ask whether the sender has a reason to write |
| 4. An odd sender address | Check that the address contains the organisation's name, spelled correctly |
| 5. A tempting or alarming subject | Be wary of "refund" or "security alert" |
| 6. A suspicious appearance | Blurry, pixelated or outdated logos |
| 7. No personalisation | Vague greetings such as "Dear customer" |
| 8. An unusual request | Inconsistent with the sender's usual messages |
| 9. A request for confidential information | No legitimate organisation asks for a code or password by message |
| 10. A tempting or worrying message | Unexpected prize, urgency, threat of account closure |
| 11. Surprising language mistakes | Typos, machine-translated phrasing, broken accented characters |
| 12. Pressure to click a link or open an attachment | Hover over the link to see the real address |

Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr notes that the language quality of phishing attempts keeps improving. The absence of mistakes is therefore no guarantee.

The site also points out that knowing the sender's address is not an absolute sign of trust: it may have been spoofed. In a business, any unusual request to make a transfer or change bank details, even from a known address, should be checked through another channel.

## Habits that help a business avoid phishing

Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr's fact sheet sets out seven protective habits:

1. **Never share sensitive information** by message or phone: no public authority or serious company asks for bank details or passwords by message.
2. **Hover over links before clicking** to display the real address, or use a bookmark you created yourself.
3. **Check the website address** in the browser: a single character can be changed to deceive.
4. **Contact the organisation directly** if in doubt, to confirm the message or call.
5. **Use different, complex passwords** for each site and app.
6. **Check the date and time of the last login** to the account when the site allows it.
7. **Enable two-factor authentication** whenever it is offered.

The fifth habit is easier to keep with a dedicated tool: the comparison of the [best business password manager]({{< relref "best-business-password-manager.md" >}}) covers solutions designed for teams.

### Measures to take at company level

Individual habits are complemented by collective measures:

- **Raise team awareness** with real examples of messages received.
- **Set a double-check rule** for any transfer or bank detail change requested by email.
- **Name a contact person** to alert in case of doubt or a click, without blame, so the team can react quickly.
- **Protect the company's domain** against spoofing with SPF, DKIM and DMARC records, covered in the guide to [creating a professional email address]({{< relref "create-professional-email-address.md" >}}).

These measures are part of the [small business cybersecurity best practices]({{< relref "small-business-cybersecurity-best-practices.md" >}}), which also cover backups and updates.

## What to do if an employee clicked on a phishing link

Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr recommends several actions after a phishing attempt:

| Situation | Recommended action |
|---|---|
| A password was entered | Change it immediately on the service concerned and wherever it was reused |
| Payment details were shared, or suspicious debits appear | Ask the bank to block the card or account immediately |
| The message is still available | Keep it as evidence |
| Identity theft or fraudulent debits observed | File a complaint with the police, the gendarmerie, or in writing with the public prosecutor |
| Doubt about the message received | Contact the organisation concerned directly |

In a business, the person who clicked should alert the IT manager or provider without delay, so they can check access, close open sessions and review the mailbox.

For advice, the French Interior Ministry's Info Escroqueries platform answers on 0 805 805 817, a free call and service, Monday to Friday from 9 am to 6:30 pm. Individuals can also get support from a France Victimes association on 116 006.

## Where to report a phishing email, text message or website in France

| Type of content | Where to report it |
|---|---|
| Suspicious email | Signal Spam, which works with the CNIL (the French data protection authority) |
| Suspicious text or MMS message | 33700, by text or on its platform (free service) |
| Phishing website | Phishing Initiative, which blocks the site's address |
| Organisation being impersonated | Alert it directly |

Reporting helps identify senders and block fraudulent websites for other recipients. Never reply to the suspicious message.

## What French law says about phishing

Phishing attackers face several offences, listed by Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr:

| Offence | Legal text | Maximum penalty |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Article 313-1 of the Criminal Code | 5 years in prison and a €375,000 fine |
| Fraudulent collection of personal data | Article 226-18 of the Criminal Code | 5 years and €300,000 |
| Unauthorised access to a computer system | Article 323-1 of the Criminal Code | 3 years and €100,000, 5 years and €150,000 if data was altered or deleted |
| Counterfeiting and fraudulent use of a means of payment | Articles L163-3 and L163-4 of the Monetary and Financial Code | 7 years and €750,000 |
| Identity theft | Article 226-4-1 of the Criminal Code | 1 year and €15,000 |

These penalties are those shown on the fact sheet consulted on 16 November 2026.
